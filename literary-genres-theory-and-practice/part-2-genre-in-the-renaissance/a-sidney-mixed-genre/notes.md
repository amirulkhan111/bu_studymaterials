# a) Sidney's Views on Mixed Genre

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part II: Genre in the Renaissance*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 1–7 of 30 (Part II)*
*Primary source: Sir Philip Sidney, **The Defence of Poesy** (also called *An Apology for Poetry*), written c. 1579–1580, published 1595 (public domain)*

---

## Hour 1: Who Was Sidney, and What Is "The Defence of Poesy"?

Sir Philip Sidney (1554–1586) was an English courtier, soldier, and poet. He wrote *The Defence of Poesy* partly as a response to attacks on poetry and drama by Puritan critics of his time (most famously Stephen Gosson's *The School of Abuse*, which accused poets of corrupting morals). Sidney's essay is one of the first major works of English literary criticism, and it works by combining classical authority — he leans heavily on Aristotle and Horace, the very thinkers covered in Part I — with direct commentary on the actual English plays being performed in his own day.

This makes Sidney a useful bridge for this course: he is not inventing a new theory of genre from scratch. He is *applying* the classical framework we studied in Part I (imitation, decorum, unity of plot) to judge contemporary Elizabethan drama — and much of what he says about "mixed genre" only makes sense as an application of that inherited framework.

---

## Hour 2: Sidney's Classification — Tragicomedy as a Legitimate "Coupled Kind"

Before criticizing anything, Sidney first classifies the kinds of poetry that exist, and he explicitly acknowledges that genres can be combined:

> "Now, in his parts, kinds, or species, as you list to term them, it is to be noted that some poesies have coupled together two or three kinds; as the tragical and comical, whereupon is risen the tragi-comical."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** Sidney is not, in principle, opposed to mixing genres. He states plainly, almost as a neutral fact of literary history, that some poets have combined the tragic and the comic kinds, and that this combination is what "tragi-comedy" *is*. This matters, because it means Sidney's later, much harsher criticism of "mongrel tragi-comedy" (Hour 5) is not a blanket rejection of mixed genre as a category — it is a complaint about how badly it is being *done* in the plays of his own day.

---

## Hour 3: The Unities — Violating the Rule of Place

Sidney's specific complaints about English drama begin with the classical "unities" of place and time — rules he traces back to Aristotle (see Part I, Topic a, on unity of plot):

> "Where the stage should always represent but one place, and the uttermost time presupposed in it should be, both by Aristotle's precept and common reason, but one day, there is both many days and many places inartificially imagined."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

He then gives a vivid, almost comic example of how English stages jump between locations with no consistency:

> "Now ye shall have three ladies walk to gather flowers, and then we must believe the stage to be a garden. By and by we hear news of shipwreck in the same place, and then we are to blame if we accept it not for a rock."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** Sidney is mocking plays that ask the audience to imagine the same patch of stage as a garden one moment and a rocky coastline the next, with nothing marking the change except the actors' words. For Sidney, this isn't just clumsy staging — it's a failure of the same principle of *unity* that Aristotle required of a well-built plot (Part I, Topic a): if the setting can shift arbitrarily, the represented action isn't really a single, coherent whole.

---

## Hour 4: The Unities — Violating the Rule of Time, and the Verdict on *Gorboduc*

Sidney is equally troubled by plays that compress years of events into the length of a single performance:

> "Two young princes fall in love; after many traverses she is got with child, delivered of a fair boy, he is lost, groweth a man, falleth in love, and is ready to get another child, — and all this in two hours' space."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** in the space of a two-hour play, Sidney complains, English drama routinely shows a child being born, growing into an adult, and having children of his own — a timeline that should span decades. This breaks the audience's ability to believe in the action as one continuous, plausible sequence.

Even the English play Sidney respects most, *Gorboduc* (by Thomas Sackville and Thomas Norton), does not escape this criticism. He praises its language and moral seriousness but still finds it guilty of the same structural flaw: he judges it "faulty both in place and time." **In plain terms:** Sidney holds even the best English tragedy of his day to the same classical standard — and even it fails the test, showing just how demanding, and how consistently applied, his standard of dramatic unity actually is.

---

## Hour 5: "Mongrel Tragi-Comedy" — Mixing Kings and Clowns

Sidney's most famous and most cutting criticism targets plays that mix serious, high-status characters with low comic figures purely for entertainment, without any real dramatic justification:

> "Mingling kings and clowns, not because the matter so carrieth it, but thrust in the clown by head and shoulders to play a part in majestical matters with neither decency nor discretion."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** Sidney's complaint is not that a king and a clown can never appear in the same play — his objection is to mixing them carelessly, "by head and shoulders," when the story itself gives no real reason for a comic figure to be there. The clown is shoved into serious, "majestical" scenes purely for cheap laughs, with (in his key words) **"neither decency nor discretion"** — no fittingness, and no judgment about whether it belongs. Notice how closely this echoes Horace's *decorum* from Part I, Topic (b): the same idea that an element must actually *belong* with its surrounding material, not just be inserted because it might entertain.

---

## Hour 6: Why the Mixing Fails — Neither Effect Is Achieved

Sidney then explains, in strictly practical terms, *why* this kind of careless mixing is a failure of craft, not just a matter of taste:

> "So as neither the admiration and commiseration, nor the right sportfulness, is by their mongrel tragi-comedy obtained."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** tragedy is supposed to produce "admiration and commiseration" — awe and pity (compare Aristotle's catharsis of pity and fear, Part I, Topic a) — while comedy is supposed to produce "right sportfulness," genuine, well-aimed humor. Sidney's argument is that when the two are mixed without discipline, a play achieves *neither* effect properly: the comic interruptions undercut the tragedy's seriousness, and the tragic weight around them undercuts the comedy's lightness. The result, in his memorable phrase, is a "mongrel" — a mixed-breed with no clear identity — rather than a genuinely new, coherent form.

---

## Hour 7: Synthesis — Sidney as Inheritor of Classical Decorum

Read together, Sidney's remarks on mixed genre are best understood not as an isolated English opinion, but as a direct continuation of the classical framework studied in Part I:

- Like **Aristotle**, Sidney insists on a unified, coherent action (Part I, Topic a) — which is why he objects so strongly to English drama's careless jumps in place and time.
- Like **Horace**, Sidney judges elements by whether they are *fitting* — decorum (Part I, Topic b) — which is exactly his complaint about clowns being "thrust in... with neither decency nor discretion."
- Yet Sidney is **not** a simple rule-enforcer who bans mixed genre outright: his own classification admits that "tragi-comical" is a legitimate coupling of kinds. His real target is *undisciplined* mixing — genre-blending done "not because the matter so carrieth it," but for cheap effect.

In other words, Sidney's "views on mixed genre" are double-sided: mixed genre is theoretically possible and even already named and classified (tragicomedy), but in practice, most English mixing he has seen fails the classical tests of unity and decorum he inherited from Aristotle and Horace. This tension — mixed genre as a legitimate *category* versus mixed genre as it is *badly executed* in contemporary plays — is exactly what later Renaissance writers (including the defenders of tragicomedy covered in Topic c of this Part) will have to answer.

## Quick Recap

- Sidney's *Defence of Poesy* applies the classical framework from Part I (Aristotle, Horace) directly to Elizabethan drama.
- Sidney's classification of poetic kinds explicitly recognizes tragicomedy as a legitimate "coupled kind" — mixed genre is not rejected in principle.
- Sidney criticizes English plays for violating the unities of place and time, tracing this rule to "Aristotle's precept."
- Even *Gorboduc*, the English play Sidney most admires, is judged "faulty both in place and time."
- His sharpest attack targets "mongrel tragi-comedy": mixing kings and clowns "with neither decency nor discretion."
- His practical objection: careless mixing achieves "neither the admiration and commiseration, nor the right sportfulness" — neither genre's proper effect is delivered.
- Sidney's standard is essentially classical decorum (Part I, Topic b) applied to English Renaissance drama.
