# b) The Views of the Renaissance Grammarians on Genre (Gascoigne & Ascham)

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part II: Genre in the Renaissance*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 8–14 of 30 (Part II)*
*Primary sources: Roger Ascham, **The Schoolmaster** (1570); George Gascoigne, **Certain Notes of Instruction Concerning the Making of Verse or Rhyme in English** (1575) — both public domain*

---

## Hour 8: Who Were Ascham and Gascoigne?

Roger Ascham (1515–1568) was a scholar and royal tutor (to the young Princess Elizabeth, later Elizabeth I). His *Schoolmaster* is primarily a book about *education* — how to teach Latin to boys — but it also contains strong opinions about which books English readers should and shouldn't read, and about English verse itself.

George Gascoigne (c. 1542–1577) was a soldier and poet. His *Certain Notes of Instruction* (1575) is generally considered the first English-language treatise specifically about *how to write English verse* — practical advice on invention, word choice, and metre, aimed at working poets rather than students of Latin.

Where Sidney (Topic a) writes as a courtier defending poetry's value and judging drama by classical standards of unity and decorum, Ascham and Gascoigne write more like **grammarians and teachers** — one concerned with what young readers should be exposed to, the other with the nuts and bolts of composing a line of English verse well. Together they show that Renaissance genre theory wasn't only debated by courtiers like Sidney; it was also being worked out at the level of the classroom and the writing desk.

---

## Hour 9: Ascham's Method — Imitation and "Double Translation"

Ascham's educational philosophy rests on **imitation**: the belief that students learn to write well by very closely studying and reproducing the style of the best authors (for him, chiefly Cicero). His signature classroom technique, still famous today, is "double translation":

> "After this, the child must take a paper book, and sitting in some place where no man shall prompt him, by himself let him translate into English his former lesson. Then, showing it to his master, let the master take from him his Latin book, and pausing an hour at the least, then let the child translate his own English into Latin again, in another paper book."
> — Ascham, *The Schoolmaster*

**In plain terms:** a student first translates a Latin passage into English, then — without looking at the original — translates their own English back into Latin. Comparing this second attempt against the original author's actual Latin shows the student exactly where their instincts diverge from a master stylist's. Although this method is about Latin prose, not English poetic genre directly, it reveals Ascham's core assumption: good writing is learned by disciplined, repeated imitation of proven models — the same assumption that underlies his opinions about which *English* books are worth imitating (Hour 10) and which English verse-forms are worth using (Hour 11).

---

## Hour 10: Ascham's Verdict on English Romance — *Morte Arthure*

Ascham is not neutral about native English storytelling. He singles out Sir Thomas Malory's *Morte d'Arthur* — the great medieval English prose romance about King Arthur's knights — for harsh criticism on moral grounds:

> "The whole pleasure of which book standeth in two special points: in open manslaughter and bold bawdry. In which book those be counted the noblest knights that do kill most men without any quarrel, and commit foulest adulteries by subtlest shifts: as Sir Launcelot with the wife of King Arthur his master; Sir Tristram with the wife of King Mark his uncle; Sir Lamerock with the wife of King Lot, that was his own aunt."
> — Ascham, *The Schoolmaster*

**In plain terms:** Ascham is not making a technical, form-based judgment about the romance as a genre (compare Aristotle's structural criteria in Part I) — he is making a *moral* judgment, arguing that the entire pleasure of this kind of book comes from violence and adultery dressed up as chivalric heroism. This matters for genre theory because it shows a Renaissance grammarian actively working to exclude an entire native English genre (romance) from the curriculum, on the grounds that its content corrupts readers — a very different kind of objection from Sidney's complaint about undisciplined mixing (Topic a) or Aristotle's complaints about plot structure (Part I).

---

## Hour 11: Ascham on English Rhyme vs. Classical Metre

Ascham also has strong opinions about the *form* of English verse itself, preferring the quantitative metre of classical Latin and Greek poetry over English rhyme, which he regards as a foreign, degraded import:

> "[Rude beggarly rhyming was] brought first into Italy by Gothes and Hunnes, when all good verses and all good learning too were destroyed by them, and after carried into France and Germany, and at last received into England by men of excellent wit indeed, but of small learning and less judgement in that behalf."
> — Ascham, *The Schoolmaster*

**In plain terms:** Ascham traces rhyme back to the barbarian invasions that destroyed classical civilization, implying that rhyme itself is a mark of cultural decline compared to the quantitative metres of Greek and Latin verse. He credits the English poets who adopted rhyme with "excellent wit" but faults them for "small learning" — meaning they didn't know or didn't apply the superior classical alternative. This is a genre-adjacent argument about *form*: for Ascham, the very building blocks of English poetry (rhyme itself) are already a step down from the classical models English writers should be imitating.

## Hour 12: Gascoigne's "Certain Notes of Instruction" — Invention First

Gascoigne opens his practical guide to English verse-writing with a claim about priorities: before anything else, a poem needs a genuine idea behind it.

> "The first and most necessary point that ever I found meet to be considered in making of a delectable poem is this: to ground it upon some fine invention."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction*

**In plain terms:** "invention" here means the underlying idea, conceit, or angle of the poem — not decoration, not clever wordplay, but the actual thought the poem exists to express. For Gascoigne, technique (metre, rhyme, word choice) only matters in service of a genuinely good idea; a technically skillful poem built on a weak or absent idea isn't worth much.

He also advises restraint with archaic or unusual vocabulary:

> "Also, as much as may be, eschew strange words, or *obsoleta et inusitata* [obsolete and unusual], unless the theme do give just occasion."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction*

**In plain terms:** don't reach for old-fashioned or unfamiliar words just to sound impressive — unless the subject itself genuinely calls for that kind of language. Notice the decorum logic here, the same principle from Horace and Sidney (Part I, Topic b; Part II, Topic a): a stylistic choice is only justified if it actually *fits* what is being written about.

---

## Hour 13: Gascoigne — Sense Over Sound

Gascoigne repeatedly warns poets against letting the demands of rhyme distort their meaning:

> "Do you always hold your first determined invention, and do rather search the bottom of your brains for apt words, than change good reason for rumbling rhyme."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction*

**In plain terms:** if you can't find the right rhyme, don't twist your meaning to force one — work harder to find the *right word* that keeps both your sense and your sound intact. Meaning should never be sacrificed just to make two line-endings match.

He makes a related point about surface-level cleverness without real substance:

> "It is not enough to roll in pleasant words... unless the invention have in it also *aliquid salis* [something of wit/salt]."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction*

**In plain terms:** smooth, pretty-sounding language is not enough on its own — a poem needs actual wit or substance behind it, not just decorative sound. Taken together, Hours 12 and 13 show Gascoigne applying a consistent standard: technique (word choice, rhyme, sound) must always serve invention and sense, never the reverse.

---

## Hour 14: Gascoigne on Verse Forms, and Synthesis

Gascoigne's treatise is also notable for offering one of the earliest English attempts to formally define specific verse forms, including the sonnet — a form this course will study in full in Topic (d):

> "I can best allow to call those sonnets which are of fourteen lines, every line containing ten syllables. The first twelve do rhyme in staves of four lines by cross metre, and the last two, rhyming together, do conclude the whole."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction*

**In plain terms:** even at this early stage, an English grammarian-poet is already trying to pin down exact formal rules — line count, syllable count, rhyme pattern — for a genre that would go on to dominate English lyric poetry (see Topic d).

**Synthesis:** Ascham and Gascoigne approach genre from the classroom and the writing desk rather than the courtroom of literary debate. Ascham's concerns are pedagogical and moral: which books (and which verse forms) are fit to imitate and teach. Gascoigne's concerns are practical and technical: how to actually build a good English poem, line by line. Yet both, like Sidney and like Aristotle and Horace before them (Part I), keep returning to the same underlying idea: that good writing is a matter of *fittingness* — words fit to sense, style fit to subject, and (for Ascham) genre fit to a reader's moral education. Renaissance genre theory, across courtiers and grammarians alike, is built on this same inherited classical foundation, even when the writers disagree sharply about which specific books or forms pass the test.

## Quick Recap

- Ascham and Gascoigne represent the "grammarian" strand of Renaissance criticism — classroom- and craft-focused, as opposed to Sidney's courtier-style defense of poetry.
- Ascham's "double translation" method reflects his broader belief that good writing is learned through disciplined imitation of the best authors.
- Ascham condemns Malory's *Morte d'Arthur* on moral grounds: its pleasure lies in "open manslaughter and bold bawdry."
- Ascham prefers classical quantitative metre to English rhyme, which he traces to the "Gothes and Hunnes" and calls "rude beggarly rhyming."
- Gascoigne's *Certain Notes of Instruction* insists a poem must be grounded in "some fine invention" before anything else.
- Gascoigne warns against archaic words used without real justification, and against sacrificing sense for the sake of rhyme.
- Gascoigne offers an early formal definition of the sonnet (14 lines, 10 syllables each, specific rhyme pattern) — previewing Topic (d).

## Review Questions

2. Why does Ascham prefer classical quantitative metre to English rhyme? Do you find his reasoning convincing?
3. Explain Gascoigne's idea of "invention" and how it relates to his advice about not sacrificing "good reason" for "rumbling rhyme."
