---

## Hour 15: The Problem Tragicomedy Has to Answer

In Topic (a), we saw Sidney attack contemporary English drama for "mongrel tragi-comedy" — mixing kings and clowns "with neither decency nor discretion," so that a play achieves "neither the admiration and commiseration, nor the right sportfulness" of proper tragedy or comedy. Yet Sidney also admitted, in the same breath, that "the tragical and comical" had already "coupled together" to produce something called the "tragi-comical" as a recognized kind.

This sets up the central question of this topic: **can genre-mixing ever be done *well* — with real rules, not just careless improvisation?** Renaissance theorists across Europe, especially in Italy, spent decades trying to answer exactly that question, working out a *deliberate*, *theorized* version of tragicomedy meant to escape Sidney's charge of being a "mongrel."

---

## Hour 16: Guarini's Defense — Tragicomedy as a Genuine Third Kind

The most influential defender of tragicomedy was the Italian poet and critic **Giovanni Battista Guarini** (1538–1612), author of the pastoral tragicomedy *Il Pastor Fido* ("The Faithful Shepherd") and, later, a theoretical defense of the form, the *Compendio della Poesia Tragicomica* ("Compendium of Tragicomic Poetry," 1601).

Guarini was writing against critics (in Italy, chiefly Jason Denores) who argued, in essentially the same spirit as Sidney, that mixing tragedy and comedy violated classical rules and produced an incoherent, rule-breaking hybrid. Guarini's central counter-argument, well documented across modern scholarship on Renaissance poetics, was that tragicomedy is not a careless mixture but a **new, coherent genre with its own internal logic**: it takes the serious, elevated subject matter and the emotional weight of tragedy, and combines it with comedy's ability to end happily and to include ordinary, lower-status characters — while deliberately *removing* tragedy's most dangerous ingredient (actual on-stage death) and comedy's most damaging one (indecent, morally corrosive humor). The result, in his argument, is a form that gives audiences serious emotional engagement without the harm of either genre taken to its extreme.

*(Note: because Guarini wrote in Italian and a free, publicly verifiable English translation of his exact wording could not be confirmed for this lecture, this section presents his argument in careful summary rather than direct quotation — consistent with this course's rule against quoting text that cannot be verified verbatim.)*

## Hour 17: Why "Taking the Best of Both" Is a Different Claim Than Sidney's

It's worth being precise about how Guarini's position differs from what Sidney was attacking. Sidney's complaint (Topic a) was about plays that mixed kings and clowns "not because the matter so carrieth it" — i.e., mixing with no structural reason, purely to entertain. Guarini's tragicomedy is not that: it proposes a specific, defensible *architecture* for a mixed play — serious plot and elevated characters (from tragedy) combined with comic devices and a happy ending (from comedy) — rather than random alternation between high and low material.

**In plain terms:** the difference is between accidental mixing (a clown randomly shoved into a tragic scene for a laugh, which is what Sidney condemns) and *structural* mixing (a play deliberately built, from the ground up, to combine tragic stakes with a comic resolution). Guarini's whole project is to show that the second kind of mixing can have its own decorum — its own internal fittingness — even though it isn't pure tragedy or pure comedy.

## Hour 18: Fletcher's Definition — What Tragicomedy Is *Not*

The clearest and most quoted English statement of what tragicomedy actually *is* comes from the dramatist **John Fletcher**, in his preface "To the Reader" prefixed to his pastoral tragicomedy *The Faithful Shepherdess* (1608). Fletcher wrote the preface because the play had confused its first audience, who expected something quite different from the word "tragicomedy." His definition works by first ruling out a common misunderstanding:

> "A tragicomedy is not so called in respect of mirth and killing, but in respect it wants deaths, which is enough to make it no tragedy, yet brings some near it, which is enough to make it no comedy."
> — Fletcher, preface to *The Faithful Shepherdess*

**In plain terms:** Fletcher is correcting a popular misconception — that "tragicomedy" simply means alternating between funny scenes and violent, deadly ones. Instead, he defines it by *absence and proximity*: a tragicomedy has no actual deaths (which is what disqualifies it from being a true tragedy), but it comes close enough to death — real danger, real stakes — that it can't be mistaken for a lighthearted comedy either. The genre is defined by *how close it gets to tragedy without crossing into it*, not by literally combining comic and tragic scenes.

---

## Hour 19: Why This Definition Matters

This is a genuinely useful theoretical move, because it gives tragicomedy a precise, testable boundary rather than a vague "mixture." **In plain terms:** under Fletcher's definition, a play doesn't become a tragicomedy just because it has both jokes and a sword fight in it — it becomes a tragicomedy specifically because it creates the *threat* of tragic outcomes (a character in mortal danger, a seemingly fatal situation) and then resolves that threat without an actual death. The tension of tragedy is present; the outcome of comedy is delivered. This is precisely the kind of rule-governed distinction that answers Sidney's objection: Fletcher is not describing random tonal whiplash, but a specific structural condition a play must meet.

---

## Hour 20: Fletcher on Characters — Gods and "Mean People" Together

Fletcher's preface also addresses which *characters* belong in a tragicomedy, directly engaging with the classical decorum-of-rank principle we studied with Horace and Sidney (Part I, Topic b; Part II, Topic a):

> "A god is as lawful in this as in a tragedy, and mean people as in a comedy."
> — Fletcher, preface to *The Faithful Shepherdess*

**In plain terms:** just as tragedy is traditionally allowed to include gods and high-ranking figures, and comedy is traditionally allowed to include ordinary, low-status ("mean") people, tragicomedy is allowed to include *both* — a god and a common person can legitimately appear in the same tragicomic play. This is Fletcher directly answering the decorum-based objection: he isn't abandoning the classical rule that a character's rank should be respected (Horace's "gods should not talk like heroes," Part I, Topic b) — he is arguing that tragicomedy, as its own genre, simply has a *wider* legitimate range of ranks than either tragedy or comedy alone, because it borrows license from both.

---

## Hour 21: Tragicomedy in Practice, and Synthesis

In practice, English tragicomedy flourished above all in the popular collaborative plays of **Fletcher and Francis Beaumont**, written for the King's Men in the early seventeenth century, and it also shaped the late plays of Shakespeare (such as *The Winter's Tale* and *The Tempest*), which mix apparent tragic loss with eventual reconciliation and survival rather than death.

**Synthesis:** Tragicomedy, properly theorized by Guarini and defined precisely by Fletcher, is the Renaissance's structured answer to Sidney's complaint about "mongrel" mixing. Where Sidney condemned English drama for shoving comic and tragic material together "with neither decency nor discretion," Guarini and Fletcher both insist that a mixed form can have its *own* discretion — its own rules about how much danger to create, how far to bring characters toward death without crossing that line, and which ranks of character may legitimately appear together. Tragicomedy does not reject classical decorum (Part I, Topic b); it redefines decorum's boundaries for a genuinely new, third kind of drama, standing between the two ancient genres rather than carelessly blending them.

## Quick Recap

- Sidney (Topic a) both criticized undisciplined genre-mixing and acknowledged tragicomedy as an already-named "coupled kind."
- Guarini defended tragicomedy in Italy as a structured, deliberate genre — combining tragedy's seriousness with comedy's happy ending, while removing each genre's most dangerous or damaging element.
- Guarini's tragicomedy differs from Sidney's "mongrel" target: it proposes structural mixing with its own logic, not random, unmotivated mixing.
- Fletcher defines tragicomedy by absence and proximity: no actual deaths (so it's not tragedy), but real danger and closeness to death (so it's not comedy).
- Fletcher extends decorum-of-rank to allow both gods and "mean people" in the same tragicomic play — borrowing license from both parent genres.
- English tragicomedy flourished in Beaumont and Fletcher's collaborative plays and shaped Shakespeare's late "romances."
