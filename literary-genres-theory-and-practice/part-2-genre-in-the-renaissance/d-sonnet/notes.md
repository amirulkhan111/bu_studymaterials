## Hour 22: What Is a Sonnet? Origins in Petrarch

A sonnet is a fixed poetic form: fourteen lines, in a strict metre, following one of a small number of set rhyme schemes. The form originated in Italy and was perfected by the poet **Francesco Petrarch** (1304–1374), whose sequence of love poems for "Laura" (the *Rime Sparse*, or "Scattered Rhymes") became the model imitated across Europe for the next 250 years — including by nearly every English sonneteer covered in this topic.

The classic Italian ("Petrarchan") sonnet divides into two parts: an **octave** (eight lines, rhyming *abbaabba*) and a **sestet** (six lines, in a more variable rhyme pattern such as *cdecde*). Between them falls the **volta**, or "turn" — a shift in the poem's argument, mood, or perspective, often where a question or problem posed in the octave is answered, complicated, or reversed in the sestet. This octave/sestet structure, and the idea of a volta, is the basic architecture that every later variation of the sonnet — English and Spenserian alike — adapts or reworks.

## Hour 23: The Sonnet Arrives in English — Wyatt, Surrey, and Tottel's Miscellany

The sonnet entered English poetry through the work of **Sir Thomas Wyatt** (1503–1542) and **Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey** (1517–1547), both of whom translated and adapted Petrarch's sonnets into English in the early sixteenth century. Their poems were first widely published together in the anthology *Songes and Sonettes* (1557), commonly known as **Tottel's Miscellany** after its printer/editor Richard Tottel — the volume that effectively introduced the sonnet to a broad English readership.

Wyatt tended to preserve something closer to the Italian octave/sestet shape. Surrey, however, developed a new structural variant better suited to English, which has less natural rhyme available than Italian: three quatrains followed by a final couplet. This is the form that would later be perfected by Shakespeare and is now most often called the "Shakespearean" or "English" sonnet — even though Surrey developed its basic shape decades before Shakespeare was born.

---

## Hour 24: Gascoigne Codifies the English Form

By the time George Gascoigne wrote his *Certain Notes of Instruction* in 1575 (Part II, Topic b), the English sonnet form was established enough that he could state its rules precisely, as a fixed recipe for poets to follow:

> "I can best allow to call those sonnets which are of fourteen lines, every line containing ten syllables. The first twelve do rhyme in staves of four lines by cross metre, and the last two, rhyming together, do conclude the whole."
> — Gascoigne, *Certain Notes of Instruction* (as previously quoted in Topic b)

**In plain terms:** Gascoigne is precisely describing the English form Surrey had pioneered — fourteen ten-syllable lines, organized as three four-line "staves" with alternating ("cross") rhyme, followed by a rhyming couplet at the end. This is a grammarian doing exactly the work we saw in Topic (b): taking a form that poets were already using in practice and writing down its exact rules, so future poets have a fixed standard to follow.

## Hour 25: Three Sonnet Forms Compared

By the later sixteenth century, English poets were using several distinct sonnet structures side by side:

| Form | Structure | Rhyme scheme | Volta position |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Petrarchan (Italian)** | Octave + sestet | *abbaabba* + *cdecde* (variable) | Between octave and sestet (line 8/9) |
| **English (Surreyan/Shakespearean)** | Three quatrains + couplet | *abab cdcd efef gg* | Often before the final couplet (line 13) |
| **Spenserian** | Three interlocking quatrains + couplet | *abab bcbc cdcd ee* | Variable, often more gradual |

The **Spenserian sonnet**, used by Edmund Spenser in his sequence *Amoretti* (1595), is a distinctive English hybrid: it keeps the English three-quatrain-plus-couplet shape but *links* each quatrain to the next by repeating a rhyme sound from the previous quatrain (note how *b* appears in both quatrain one and two, and *c* in both quatrain two and three) — giving the poem a more continuous, chain-like feel than the more sharply segmented English form.

**In plain terms:** all three forms keep the same basic contract — fourteen lines, a turn of thought somewhere in the poem — but differ in how the rhymes are organized, which changes the *pacing* of that turn: sudden and clean in the Petrarchan octave/sestet split, delayed until the final couplet in the English form, and more gradually built up through interlocking quatrains in the Spenserian form.

## Hour 26: Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella* — The First Major English Sonnet Sequence

Sir Philip Sidney (already studied in Topic a for his views on mixed genre) was also the author of *Astrophil and Stella* (written c. 1582, published 1591), widely regarded as the first major sonnet **sequence** in English — a linked series of sonnets tracing one speaker's unfolding, largely unsuccessful love (Astrophil, "star-lover," for Stella, "star"). Its opening poem, Sonnet 1, is itself a poem about the difficulty of writing sonnets:

> "Loving in truth, and fain in verse my love to show,
> That the dear She might take some pleasure of my pain,
> Pleasure might cause her read, reading might make her know,
> Knowledge might pity win, and pity grace obtain,
> I sought fit words to paint the blackest face of woe,
> Studying inventions fine, her wits to entertain,
> Oft turning others' leaves, to see if thence would flow
> Some fresh and fruitful showers upon my sunburned brain.
>
> But words came halting forth, wanting Invention's stay:
> Invention, Nature's child, fled step-dame Study's blows,
> And others' feet still seemed but strangers in my way.
>
> Thus great with child to speak, and helpless in my throes,
> Biting my truant pen, beating myself for spite:
> 'Fool,' said my Muse to me, 'look in thy heart and write.'"
> — Sidney, *Astrophil and Stella*, Sonnet 1

---

## Hour 27: Reading Sidney's Sonnet 1

**In plain terms:** the speaker explains his whole strategy in the octave — he wants to write moving verse so that his beloved will read it, be moved by it, and eventually love him back (a chain of cause and effect: pleasure leads to reading, reading to knowledge, knowledge to pity, pity to love). To do this well, he searches other poets' work ("turning others' leaves") for ideas he can borrow. But in the final tercet, this strategy collapses: he calls imitation "step-dame [stepmother] Study" — cold, borrowed technique — as opposed to "Invention, Nature's child," genuine, natural inspiration. Copying other poets' methods leaves him unable to write anything real. The poem's famous final line delivers the solution as a piece of advice from his own Muse: **"Fool, look in thy heart, and write."**

Notice the volta here: this is written in the English three-quatrain-plus-couplet form (matching Gascoigne's definition and Surrey's model), but the "turn" doesn't wait for the very last couplet — it begins at line 9 ("But words came halting forth"), closer to where a Petrarchan sonnet would place its octave/sestet break. Sidney is using the English rhyme scheme with something closer to Italian pacing.

This sonnet is also a poem *about* genre theory in miniature: it dramatizes the very tension between imitation and authentic feeling that runs through this entire course, from Aristotle's account of imitation (Part I, Topic c) to Gascoigne's insistence that a poem must be grounded in real "invention," not just borrowed technique (Part II, Topic b).

## Hour 28: Sidney's Own Critique of English Sonneteers

Strikingly, in *The Defence of Poesy* (Topic a), Sidney criticizes the very kind of derivative, imitation-heavy love poetry his own Sonnet 1 warns against — suggesting he saw contemporary English sonnet-writing as largely failing to follow its own best advice:

> "So coldly they apply fiery speeches, as men that had rather read lovers' writings... than that in truth they feel those passions."
> — Sidney, *The Defence of Poesy*

**In plain terms:** Sidney complains that many English love poets write "fiery" (passionate) language in a "cold," mechanical way — imitating the outward gestures of passionate poetry (borrowed from Petrarch and his many imitators) without genuinely feeling anything themselves. They write like people who have *read about* love in other poets' work, not people who have actually experienced it. This is exactly the failure his own Muse warns Astrophil against in Sonnet 1: relying on "others' leaves" instead of looking into one's own heart.

## Hour 29: Shakespeare's Sonnets

William Shakespeare's sequence of 154 sonnets (published 1609) is the most famous body of sonnets in English, using the English (quatrains-plus-couplet) form throughout. Its most famous single poem is Sonnet 18:

> "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?
> Thou art more lovely and more temperate:
> Rough winds do shake the darling buds of May,
> And summer's lease hath all too short a date:
> Sometime too hot the eye of heaven shines,
> And often is his gold complexion dimmed;
> And every fair from fair sometime declines,
> By chance, or nature's changing course untrimmed;
> But thy eternal summer shall not fade,
> Nor lose possession of that fair thou ow'st,
> Nor shall death brag thou wander'st in his shade,
> When in eternal lines to time thou grow'st:
> So long as men can breathe, or eyes can see,
> So long lives this, and this gives life to thee."
> — Shakespeare, *Sonnets*, No. 18

**In plain terms:** the first two quatrains compare the beloved favorably to a summer's day, then list summer's flaws (rough winds, short duration, an overly hot or dimmed sun) that the beloved supposedly doesn't share. The volta arrives cleanly at line 9 ("But thy eternal summer shall not fade") — exactly where the English form's third quatrain begins — pivoting from comparison to a bold claim: the beloved's beauty will not fade, because the poem itself will preserve it forever. The final couplet delivers the poem's real argument: as long as people can read ("so long as men can breathe, or eyes can see"), this very poem keeps the beloved alive. Where Sidney's Sonnet 1 is about the *difficulty* of writing sincerely, Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 makes a confident claim about poetry's *power* — specifically, its power to defeat time itself, a recurring theme across his sequence.

## Hour 30: Synthesis — The Sonnet as the Renaissance's Naturalized New Genre

Bringing this topic together with the rest of Part II: the sonnet is arguably the clearest success story of Renaissance genre theory in action. It began as a foreign import (Italian, via Petrarch), was carried into English by translator-poets (Wyatt, Surrey), was codified into precise formal rules by a grammarian (Gascoigne, Topic b), and was then used by practicing poets (Sidney, Spenser, Shakespeare) both to follow those rules and — in Sidney's case — to interrogate the very authenticity of the genre's conventions from within a poem written in that genre.

Unlike tragicomedy (Topic c), which had to be *defended* against charges of being an illegitimate "mongrel" mixture, the sonnet faced no such crisis of legitimacy — its main developmental drama was formal (which rhyme scheme? where does the volta fall?) and personal/ethical (is this poet's borrowed passion genuine, per Sidney's own self-critique?), rather than a battle over whether the form was allowed to exist at all. Across Part II as a whole, we've now seen the full range of Renaissance genre concerns: Sidney's decorum-based objections to careless mixing (Topic a), the grammarians' classroom- and craft-based rule-making (Topic b), a genuinely new hybrid genre earning theoretical legitimacy (Topic c), and a foreign form fully naturalized into English through translation, codification, and masterful practice (Topic d).

## Quick Recap

- The sonnet originated with Petrarch: fourteen lines, an octave/sestet structure, and a "volta" (turn) between them.
- Wyatt and Surrey brought the sonnet into English via Tottel's Miscellany (1557); Surrey developed the three-quatrains-plus-couplet "English" form.
- Gascoigne (Topic b) codified this English form precisely: fourteen ten-syllable lines, three cross-rhymed quatrains, and a closing couplet.
- Three main forms exist: Petrarchan (octave/sestet), English/Shakespearean (three quatrains + couplet), and Spenserian (interlocking quatrains + couplet).
- Sidney's *Astrophil and Stella*, Sonnet 1, dramatizes the tension between imitation ("step-dame Study") and authentic feeling ("Invention, Nature's child"), ending "look in thy heart, and write."
- Sidney's *Defence of Poesy* criticizes contemporary sonneteers for applying "fiery speeches" "coldly" — imitating passion without feeling it.
- Shakespeare's Sonnet 18 uses the English form's volta (line 9) to pivot from comparison to a claim about poetry's power to preserve beauty against time.
- The sonnet's development shows Renaissance genre theory succeeding at naturalizing and codifying a foreign form, in contrast to tragicomedy's harder battle for legitimacy (Topic c).

Across Part II, the Renaissance writers we've studied never abandon the classical inheritance of Part I — imitation, decorum, unity — but they apply it to new problems: judging contemporary English drama (Sidney), teaching English composition (Ascham, Gascoigne), defending a genuinely new hybrid genre (Guarini, Fletcher), and adapting a foreign lyric form into a native English tradition (the sonneteers). Genre theory, in other words, moves from ancient Greece and Rome into a live, evolving national literature — and much of the vocabulary this course has built in Part I (decorum, unity, imitation, catharsis) turns out to still be exactly the right vocabulary for understanding it.
