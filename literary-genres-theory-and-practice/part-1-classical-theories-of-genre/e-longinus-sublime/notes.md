# e) Longinus's Concept of the Sublime

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part I: Classical Theories of Genre*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 25–30 of 30 (Part I — final topic)*
*Primary source: **On the Sublime** (*Peri Hypsous*), traditionally attributed to Longinus, trans. H. L. Havell (1890, public domain)*

---

## Hour 25: Who Was Longinus, and What Is "On the Sublime"?

*On the Sublime* is a Greek treatise on great writing, traditionally attributed to an author named "Longinus," probably writing in the 1st century CE. (Scholars have long debated the true authorship and date, and the name "Longinus" is a traditional attribution rather than a certainty — this course will refer to "Longinus" as the treatise conventionally does, without claiming more biographical certainty than we actually have.)

Unlike Aristotle (rules of tragedy/epic structure) or Horace (decorum and craft), Longinus isn't primarily interested in the *rules* of a genre at all. He is interested in a single quality — **sublimity** (Greek *hypsos*, "height" or "elevation") — that can appear in poetry, oratory, or prose, and in any genre, whenever writing achieves true greatness.

---

## Hour 26: Sublimity Is "Transport," Not Persuasion

Longinus opens by distinguishing sublime writing from ordinary effective writing by its *effect* on the audience:

> "A lofty passage does not convince the reason of the reader, but takes him out of himself."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. I

**In plain terms:** ordinary good writing (a clear argument, a well-organized speech) works by *persuading* — it convinces your reasoning mind, step by step. Sublime writing works differently: it overwhelms you, sweeps you up, and produces something closer to astonishment than agreement. You don't calmly conclude a sublime passage is good — you're seized by it.

This is a genuinely new idea in classical criticism: it isn't about following rules of structure or decorum (Topics a–b) at all — it's about a specific, almost involuntary emotional/psychological *effect* on the reader or listener.

---

## Hour 27: The Test of True Sublimity

How do you know something is genuinely sublime, rather than just momentarily impressive? Longinus gives two linked tests. First, does it survive repeated exposure?

> "When a passage is pregnant in suggestion, when it is hard, nay impossible, to distract the attention from it, and when it takes a strong and lasting hold on the memory, then we may be sure that we have lighted on the true Sublime."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. VII

**In plain terms:** true sublimity doesn't wear off — you can reread or rehear it and it keeps its grip, and you don't forget it. Second, does it hold up across different kinds of people?

> "Those words [are] truly noble and sublime which always please and please all readers. For when the same book always produces the same impression on all who read it, whatever be the difference in their pursuits, their manner of life, their aspirations, their ages, or their language, such a harmony of opposites gives irresistible authority to their favourable verdict."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. VII

**In plain terms:** if people from completely different backgrounds, ages, and walks of life all independently respond to the same passage the same way, that near-universal agreement is itself strong evidence that the passage is truly great — not just fashionable or a matter of individual taste.

---

## Hour 28: The Five Sources of Sublimity

Longinus identifies five things that, together, produce sublime writing:

> "The first and the most important is grandeur of thought... The second is a vigorous and spirited treatment of the passions... The third is a certain artifice in the employment of figures... The fourth is dignified expression... The fifth cause of sublimity, which embraces all those preceding, is majesty and elevation [of structure/composition]."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. VIII

**In plain terms**, the five sources are:
1. **Grandeur of thought** — the ability to conceive genuinely great ideas (Longinus explicitly calls this the most important of the five)
2. **Strong, inspired emotion** — real passion behind the writing, not manufactured feeling
3. **Skillful use of figures** — rhetorical/poetic devices used with real artistry
4. **Noble diction** — the right, elevated choice and arrangement of words
5. **Dignity of overall composition** — how everything is structured and arranged together

**In plain terms, overall:** notice that only the last three (figures, diction, composition) are teachable *craft* — the first two (great thought, real passion) are more about the writer's own mind and character. This is why Longinus, unlike Aristotle or Horace, keeps insisting sublimity can't be reduced to technique alone.

---

## Hour 29: Sublimity in Action — "Let There Be Light" and Ajax's Prayer

Longinus illustrates grandeur of thought with two famous examples. First, he links sublimity directly to the greatness of the mind behind it:

> "Sublimity is, so to say, the image of greatness of soul."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. IX

He then quotes, as an example of sublime simplicity, the opening of Genesis:

> "God said" — what? — "'let there be light, and there was light: let there be land, and there was.'"
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. IX, quoting Genesis

**In plain terms:** Longinus finds this sublime *because* of its plainness — an idea of pure, world-creating power, expressed with no ornament at all. Greatness of thought, not decoration, does all the work.

His second example is Homer's Ajax, praying not for safety but simply for enough daylight to fight and die honorably:

> "Almighty Sire, only from darkness save Achaia's sons; no more I ask, but give us back the day; grant but our sight, and slay us, if thou wilt."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. IX, quoting Homer, *Iliad*

**In plain terms:** Ajax doesn't beg to live — he asks only to *see*, so that if he must die, he dies fighting, not helplessly in the dark. Longinus reads the sheer scale of that request (indifference to death itself, so long as the mind/eyes can still function) as an expression of a genuinely great soul, which is exactly why the line strikes readers as sublime rather than merely sad.

## Hour 30: The Technique of Selection — Sappho's Ode

Sublimity isn't only about grand subject matter — it can also come from craft: knowing exactly which details to choose. Longinus explains this principle directly:

> "One cause of sublimity is the choice of the most striking circumstances involved in whatever we are describing, and, further, the power of afterwards combining them into one animate whole."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. X

He demonstrates it with Sappho's ode describing the physical symptoms of overwhelming love/jealousy:

> "A subtle fire through all my body inly steals; mine eyes in darkness reel and swim; strange murmurs drown my ears; with dewy damps my limbs are chilled; an icy shiver shakes my frame..."
> — *On the Sublime*, Ch. X, quoting Sappho

**In plain terms:** Sappho doesn't just say "I felt overwhelmed" — she selects a handful of extreme, even contradictory physical symptoms (burning *and* chilled, blind *and* deafened) and fuses them into a single, unified picture of total emotional overload. Longinus's point: sublimity here comes not from an inherently "big" subject (a love-pang is a small, ordinary human experience) but from the *skill of selection and combination* — proof that sublimity, while rooted partly in greatness of soul, is also a real, learnable technique.

## End of Part I — Recap Across All Five Topics

Part I has moved through five different lenses for thinking about classical genre:

| Topic | Core Question | Core Thinkers |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Tragedy & Comedy | What are the parts and purpose of these two genres? | Aristotle |
| (b) Decorum | What makes an element "fitting" to its character, age, rank, or genre? | Aristotle, Horace |
| (c) Rules vs. Representation | What does imitation actually *do* — copy us away from truth, or teach us universal truths? | Plato, Aristotle |
| (d) Epic | How does narrated, large-scale storytelling differ from staged drama? | Aristotle, Horace |
| (e) The Sublime | What quality of greatness can transport an audience, beyond any single genre's rules? | Longinus |

Notice the arc: (a)–(b) give the technical apparatus of genre (parts, decorum); (c) asks what representation itself means; (d) applies all of this to a second major genre, epic; and (e) finally steps *outside* genre altogether, asking what makes writing simply great, regardless of its genre's rules. Longinus's sublime is, in this sense, the natural capstone to Part I — a reminder that classical critics were never only rule-makers; they were also always asking what, ultimately, moves a human audience.
