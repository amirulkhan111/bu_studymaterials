# a) Aristotle's Views on Tragedy and Comedy

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part I: Classical Theories of Genre*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 1–6 of 30 (Part I)*
*Primary source: Aristotle, **Poetics**, trans. S. H. Butcher (public domain)*

---

## Hour 1: What Aristotle Means by "Imitation"

Aristotle starts the *Poetics* from one basic idea: all poetry (which for him includes tragedy, comedy, and epic) is a form of **imitation** — in Greek, *mimesis*. This just means poetry copies or represents life: human actions, characters, and feelings.

He says different poets imitate different *kinds* of people:

> "Homer, for example, makes men better than they are; Cleophon as they are; Hegemon the Thasian... worse than they are."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. II

**In plain terms:** a writer can show people as better than average, as they really are, or as worse than average. This one distinction becomes the seed of Aristotle's whole theory of genre: **tragedy imitates people better than average; comedy imitates people worse than average.**

---

## Hour 2: Where Tragedy and Comedy Came From

Aristotle also gives a (brief, speculative) history of how these two genres began:

> "Tragedy — as also Comedy — was at first mere improvisation. The one originated with the authors of the Dithyramb, the other with those of the phallic songs."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. IV

**In plain terms:** neither genre was invented on purpose by one person. Both grew out of informal, group performance traditions — tragedy out of hymns sung to the god Dionysus (the dithyramb), comedy out of rowdier festival songs. Over time these performances became more structured and turned into the formal genres we know.

---

## Hour 3: The Definition of Tragedy

This is the single most famous sentence in the *Poetics*:

> "Tragedy, then, is an imitation of an action that is serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude; in language embellished with each kind of artistic ornament, the several kinds being found in separate parts of the play; in the form of action, not of narrative; through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. VI

**Breaking this down in plain language:**

| Phrase | What it means |
|---|---|
| "imitation of an action" | Tragedy shows people *doing* things, not just describing them |
| "serious, complete, and of a certain magnitude" | The story matters, has a full shape (not a fragment), and is substantial — not trivial |
| "language embellished with artistic ornament" | It uses heightened, poetic language, including song, at different points |
| "in the form of action, not of narrative" | It is *performed/acted*, not told by a narrator (this separates drama from epic) |
| "through pity and fear... proper purgation of these emotions" | Watching the tragedy stirs up pity and fear in the audience, and somehow relieves/clears out those feelings — this is the famous idea of **catharsis** |

## Hour 4: The Six Parts of Tragedy

Aristotle breaks tragedy down into six components:

> "Every Tragedy, therefore, must have six parts, which parts determine its quality — namely, Plot, Character, Diction, Thought, Spectacle, Song."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. VI

**In plain terms**, these are:
1. **Plot** — the arrangement of events/story
2. **Character** — the people in the story and their moral qualities
3. **Diction** — the actual choice of words and verse
4. **Thought** — the reasoning/ideas the characters express
5. **Spectacle** — the visual staging (costumes, scenery)
6. **Song** — the musical element

Aristotle then ranks them by importance — and his ranking often surprises students, because he puts *plot* above *character*:

> "But most important of all is the structure of the incidents... Character holds the second place."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. VI

**In plain terms:** for Aristotle, tragedy is primarily about *what happens* (the plot), not primarily about *who it happens to* (the characters' personalities). A good story with thin characters beats vivid characters with no real story.

---

## Hour 5: What Makes a Good Plot — and What Is Comedy?

**Plot structure.** A tragedy's plot must be a whole, unified action:

> "A whole is that which has a beginning, a middle, and an end... A well constructed plot, therefore, must neither begin nor end at haphazard."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. VII

**In plain terms:** events must be connected by cause and effect, not just be a random sequence — remove or move one incident, and the whole story should fall apart. This is the root of what later critics call "unity of action."

**Comedy**, by contrast, imitates people who are worse than average — but not in every sense of "bad":

> "Comedy is, as we have said, an imitation of characters of a lower type — not, however, in the full sense of the word bad, the ludicrous being merely a subdivision of the ugly. It consists in some defect or ugliness which is not painful or destructive."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. V

**In plain terms:** comic characters are flawed, foolish, or lesser in some way — but their flaws are harmless. A comic flaw makes us laugh, not wince; if it caused real pain or damage, it would stop being funny and become tragic or simply painful instead. (Example, not from Aristotle: a comic mask showing a distorted, ugly face is still funny because it isn't actually causing anyone pain.)

## Hour 6: The Tragic Hero, Hamartia, and Catharsis

Aristotle asks: what kind of person should the tragic hero be, to make the plot work? He rules out two extremes — a thoroughly good person falling into misfortune (which he says is simply "shocking," not tragic) and a thoroughly bad person falling into misfortune (which he says is not pitiable at all, just deserved). What's left is:

> "There remains, then, the character between these two extremes — that of a man who is not eminently good and just, yet whose misfortune is brought about not by vice or depravity, but by some error or frailty."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XIII

**In plain terms:** the ideal tragic hero is a basically decent, "average or above" person — not a saint, not a villain — who falls from good fortune to bad fortune because of a mistake or flaw, not because they were evil. This mistake/flaw is what later critics label with the Greek word **hamartia** (often loosely translated "tragic flaw," though it can also just mean an error of judgment, not a moral defect).

**Catharsis**, mentioned already in the definition of tragedy, is worth isolating on its own:

> "...through pity and fear effecting the proper purgation of these emotions."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. VI

**In plain terms:** by watching a decent person suffer through no real fault of their own (or through an understandable error), the audience feels pity (for the hero) and fear (that it could happen to them too). Experiencing these emotions in the safe space of the theatre, Aristotle suggests, "purges" or releases them — the audience leaves emotionally lighter, not heavier. This is one of the most debated ideas in all of literary theory; Aristotle himself does not fully spell out the mechanism, only asserts the effect.

## Quick Recap

- Tragedy imitates people *better* than average; comedy imitates people *worse* than average (but harmlessly so).
- Both genres grew out of informal group performance (dithyramb → tragedy; phallic songs → comedy).
- Tragedy = imitation of a serious, complete, substantial action, in the form of action (not narration), producing catharsis of pity and fear.
- Tragedy has six parts: Plot, Character, Diction, Thought, Spectacle, Song — with **Plot ranked above Character**.
- A good plot is a unified whole with a beginning, middle, and end.
- Comedy's flaws are "ugly" but painless — that's what makes them funny rather than tragic.
- The ideal tragic hero is an ordinary-to-good person who falls through **hamartia** (an error/frailty), not through villainy.
