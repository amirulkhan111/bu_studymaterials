# b) The Emphasis on Decorum in Aristotle & Horace

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part I: Classical Theories of Genre*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 7–12 of 30 (Part I)*
*Primary sources: Aristotle, **Poetics**, trans. S. H. Butcher; Horace, **Ars Poetica** ("The Art of Poetry"), trans. John Conington (both public domain)*

---

## Hour 7: What Does "Decorum" Mean?

**Decorum** simply means *fittingness* — doing/saying/showing the right thing, in the right way, for the right occasion, character, or genre. It is not primarily a moral rule; it's an aesthetic and logical one: does this word, action, or style actually *belong* with this subject, this speaker, this kind of work?

Both Aristotle and Horace treat decorum as one of the most important tests of good writing — a badly-fitted element (a low word in a lofty speech, a god talking like a servant, a comic plot told in tragic verse) breaks the illusion and weakens the work, even if each piece is well made on its own.

---

## Hour 8: Aristotle's Four Aims for Character — "Propriety"

In *Poetics* Ch. XV, Aristotle lists four things a poet must aim at when creating a character. The second of these is decorum by name (translated "propriety"):

> "First, and most important, it must be good... The second thing to aim at is propriety. There is a type of manly valor; but valor in a woman, or unscrupulous cleverness, is inappropriate."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XV

**In plain terms:** a character's qualities must suit *who* they are — their sex, age, social rank, and role in the story. Aristotle's own (dated, historically-specific) example is that a certain kind of "manly courage" would be an odd, unfitting quality to give a female character *as he understood the norms of his time* — the general principle, stripped of his specific example, is: **a trait can be admirable in itself and still be the wrong trait for this particular character.**

He also gives a fourth aim, **consistency**, which is closely linked to propriety:

> "The fourth point is consistency: for though the subject of the imitation, who suggested the type, be inconsistent, still he must be consistently inconsistent."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XV

**In plain terms:** even a character who is deliberately unpredictable must be unpredictable in a *recognizable, stable* way throughout the work — the inconsistency itself becomes the character's consistent trait.

Finally, Aristotle ties character speech/action to a governing rule:

> "A person of a given character should speak or act in a given way, by the rule either of necessity or of probability."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XV

**In plain terms:** what a character does or says must follow logically from who they are — not from what's convenient for the plot.

## Hour 9: Horace's Opening Image — the Monster and the "Unified" Work

Horace's *Ars Poetica* opens with a comic, grotesque image to introduce the whole idea of fittingness:

> "Suppose some painter, as a tour de force, / Should couple head of man with neck of horse..."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** imagine a painting that stitches a human head onto a horse's neck, with random feathers and mismatched limbs added on — you'd laugh, because nothing about it belongs together. Horace's point: a poem that mixes incompatible tones, styles, or parts is exactly as absurd as this painting, even if each individual "part" (the head, the neck, the feathers) is well drawn. **Decorum is what holds the parts of a work together as one coherent whole**, the same way Aristotle's "unity of plot" (see Topic a) held a story's events together.

---

## Hour 10: Matching Style to Genre — "Each Has Its Place Allotted"

Horace insists each genre has its own proper register of language, and warns against mixing them:

> "A comic subject steadily declines / To be related in high tragic lines."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

> "Each has its place allotted; each is bound / To keep it, nor invade its neighbour's ground."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** comic material sounds wrong dressed up in grand tragic verse, and vice versa — every genre has boundaries of tone and diction, and a good poet respects them rather than blurring them for effect. This is the direct Roman echo of Aristotle's genre-based thinking from Topic (a): different genres aren't just different "settings," they come with their own appropriate *style*.

---

## Hour 11: Decorum of Age — the "Ages of Man" Passage

Horace's best-known illustration of decorum in characterization is his description of how a person's temperament changes across a lifetime — and how a poet must portray each stage accurately, not interchangeably:

> "Note first the boy who just knows how to talk / And feels his feet beneath him in his walk..."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

> "The beardless youth, at last from tutor freed, / Loves playing-field and tennis, dog and steed..."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

> "His nature revolutionized, the man / Makes friends and money when and how he can..."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

> "Grey hairs have many evils: without end / The old man gathers what he dares not spend, / While, as for action, do he what he will, / 'Tis all half-hearted, spiritless, and chill: / Inert, irresolute, his neck he cranes / Into the future, grumbles, and complains..."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** a child, a reckless youth, a busy adult, and a cautious, complaining old man do not think, want, or speak alike. Decorum requires the poet to write each age *as that age actually behaves*, not to give an old man a young man's appetite for horses and games, or a boy an old man's caution. This is the same principle as Aristotle's "true to life," applied specifically to the life stage of a character.

---

## Hour 12: Decorum of Rank — "Gods Should Not Talk Like Heroes"

Horace extends the same rule to social and cosmic rank — a speaker's *status* dictates their appropriate manner of speech:

> "Gods should not talk like heroes, nor again / Impetuous youth like grave and reverend men."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** a god, a hero, an old man, and a hot-headed youth all occupy different "ranks" in a story, and each rank has a natural register of speech. Giving a god the blunt language of a common soldier, or a solemn elder the impulsiveness of a teenager, breaks decorum — it feels false, regardless of how well the individual line is written.

**Synthesis:** Aristotle names this principle "propriety" and applies it mainly to character (good, appropriate, true to life, consistent). Horace applies the very same idea more broadly — to unity of form, to genre-appropriate style, to age, and to social rank. Between them, decorum becomes one of the central tests of classical literary judgment: **not "is this good writing?" in isolation, but "is this the right writing for this particular person, moment, and kind of work?"**

---

## Quick Recap

- Decorum = fittingness: the right style/behavior/word for the right character, age, rank, or genre.
- Aristotle (*Poetics* Ch. XV): character must be good, **appropriate** (propriety), true to life, and consistent.
- Horace's monster-painting opening: a work that mixes incompatible parts is absurd, however skilled each part is on its own.
- Horace: comedy and tragedy each have their own proper style — "each has its place allotted."
- Horace's "ages of man": boy, youth, adult, and old man each have distinct, non-interchangeable temperaments a poet must respect.
- Horace: rank governs speech — gods, heroes, elders, and youths should not sound alike.
