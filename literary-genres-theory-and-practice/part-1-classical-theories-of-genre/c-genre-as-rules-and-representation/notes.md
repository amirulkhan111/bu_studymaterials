# c) Classical Understanding of Genre: Rules and Representation

*Course: Literary Genres in Theory and Practice — Part I: Classical Theories of Genre*
*Approx. lecture time: Hours 13–18 of 30 (Part I)*
*Primary sources: Plato, **Republic**, Bk. X; Aristotle, **Poetics**, Ch. IV & IX — both trans. Benjamin Jowett / S. H. Butcher (public domain)*

---

## Hour 13: Two Different Questions About Genre

So far (Topics a–b) we've looked at genre mostly as a **rulebook**: tragedy has six parts, ranked by importance; comedy and tragedy must not borrow each other's style; characters must be good, appropriate, true to life, and consistent. These are *prescriptive* — they tell a poet what to do.

But classical critics were also asking a second, deeper question that isn't about rules at all: **what is a genre actually doing when it imitates life?** This is the question of **representation** (*mimesis*). It's less "what are the parts of tragedy?" and more "what kind of truth, if any, does imitating human action give us?"

This topic looks at that second question — mainly through the disagreement between Plato and Aristotle, the two foundational classical voices on it.

---

## Hour 14: Plato's Suspicion — The Three Beds

In *Republic*, Book X, Plato (through Socrates) argues that all imitative art, including poetry, is dangerously far from truth. He illustrates this with the example of a bed:

> "God, whether from choice or from necessity, made one bed in nature and one only."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

There is then the *carpenter*, who makes a physical bed based on that idea. And then there is the *painter*:

> "The painter is an imitator of that which the others make."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

Applying this to poetry, Plato concludes:

> "The tragic poet is an imitator, and therefore, like all other imitators, he is thrice removed from the king and from the truth."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

**In plain terms:** there are three "beds" — the true, ideal Bed (an idea/Form, made by God/nature), the carpenter's physical bed (a copy of the idea), and the painter's picture of a bed (a copy of the copy). A poem or play, for Plato, works the same way: it doesn't show us reality directly, it shows us a copy of a copy — and copies of copies are, by definition, further from the truth, not closer to it.

He extends this to the poet's *knowledge*:

> "The poet is like a painter who... will make a likeness of a cobbler though he understands nothing of cobbling."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

**In plain terms:** a poet can convincingly write a soldier, a doctor, or a king without actually knowing how to fight, heal, or rule — because imitation only needs to *look* right to an audience, not *be* right. For Plato this is a serious flaw, not a skill.

---

## Hour 15: Plato's Second Objection — Poetry Feeds the Emotions

Plato's objection isn't only about truth; it's also about the *effect* poetry has on an audience:

> "Poetry feeds and waters the passions instead of drying them up; she lets them rule, although they ought to be controlled."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

**In plain terms:** watching or reading tragedy makes us indulge grief, fear, and pity rather than mastering them with reason. Plato worries this trains audiences to be ruled by emotion in real life, too — the opposite of what a rational, well-governed person (or city) needs.

He sums up the whole debate in one famous line:

> "There is an ancient quarrel between philosophy and poetry."
> — Plato, *Republic*, Bk. X

**In plain terms:** this isn't Plato inventing a new complaint — he's naming a long-standing tension that already existed between philosophers (who value reasoned truth) and poets (who move people through emotion and imitation).

---

## Hour 16: Aristotle's Reply — Imitation Is Natural and Valuable

Aristotle, writing after Plato and responding implicitly to him, starts from the opposite instinct. In *Poetics* Ch. IV, he argues imitation isn't a dangerous trick — it's built into human nature:

> "Imitation is implanted in man from childhood, one difference between him and other animals being that he is the most imitative of living creatures."
> — Aristotle, *Poetics*, Ch. IV

And rather than being a route *away* from knowledge (as Plato feared), Aristotle says imitation is a route *toward* it — imitation gives pleasure because it teaches us something:

> "The reason why men enjoy seeing a likeness is, that in contemplating it they find themselves learning or inferring, and saying perhaps, 'Ah, that is he.'"
> — Aristotle, *Poetics*, Ch. IV

**In plain terms:** where Plato saw imitation as a copy that pulls us away from truth, Aristotle saw it as a natural way humans *learn* — recognizing a likeness is itself a small act of understanding, and that's why it's pleasurable, even when the subject (a corpse, a disgusting animal, a suffering hero) would be unpleasant to witness directly.

---

## Hour 17: Aristotle's Answer to "What Does Poetry Represent?" — The Universal, Not the Particular

Aristotle's strongest, most direct rebuttal to Plato's "copy of a copy" argument comes in *Poetics* Ch. IX, where he compares the poet to the historian:

> "It is not the function of the poet to relate what has happened, but what may happen — what is possible according to the law of probability or necessity."
> — Aristotle, *Poetics*, Ch. IX

> "Poetry is a more philosophical and a higher thing than history: for poetry tends to express the universal, history the particular."
> — Aristotle, *Poetics*, Ch. IX

**In plain terms:** a historian is stuck reporting one specific thing that actually happened to one specific person (a particular). A poet, by contrast, isn't limited to fact — the poet builds a plausible chain of cause and effect showing the kind of thing that *would* happen to *that kind* of person (a universal). For Aristotle, this makes poetry *more* philosophical than history, not less truthful than reality, exactly the opposite of Plato's ranking.

---

## Hour 18: Putting It Together — Genre as Rulebook AND as Representation

We can now connect this topic back to Topics (a) and (b):

| Lens | What it asks | Example from this course |
|---|---|---|
| **Genre as rules** | What structural/stylistic conventions define this genre? | Tragedy's six parts; decorum of age, rank, and style (Topics a–b) |
| **Genre as representation** | What kind of truth about human life does this genre imitate, and how? | Plato: imitation is a copy of a copy, feeding emotion. Aristotle: imitation expresses universal, probable human truths, and is a natural source of learning. |

Classical genre theory was never *only* a technical rulebook. Underneath the rules (six parts of tragedy, decorum of age and rank, unity of plot) sits a live disagreement about what imitation itself is *for*: is it a dangerous copy that leads us away from truth and self-control (Plato), or a natural, philosophical act of learning that captures universal human patterns (Aristotle)? Later genre theory across cultures and centuries keeps returning to this same fork.

---

## Quick Recap

- Classical genre theory operates on two levels at once: **rules** (structure, style, decorum) and **representation** (what imitation *is* and *does*).
- Plato (*Republic* X): all art is imitation of imitation — "thrice removed" from truth (the three beds); poetry feeds emotion instead of controlling it; philosophy and poetry have an "ancient quarrel."
- Aristotle (*Poetics* IV, IX): imitation is natural to humans and a source of learning and pleasure; poetry shows the universal/probable, not just the particular/factual — making it "more philosophical" than history.
- The rules covered in Topics (a) and (b) are the practical, technical side of a much bigger theoretical question about what representation itself accomplishes.
