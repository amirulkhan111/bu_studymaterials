## Hour 19: What Kind of Imitation Is Epic?

Back in Topic (a) we saw Aristotle define tragedy partly by *how* it imitates: "in the form of action, not of narrative." Epic is the genre that takes the opposite path. In *Poetics* Ch. III, Aristotle sets out three possible modes any imitative poet can use:

> "The poet may imitate by narration — in which case he can either take another personality as Homer does, or speak in his own person, unchanged — or he may present all his characters as living and moving before us."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. III

**In plain terms:** you can tell a story (i) as a narrator speaking as someone else, (ii) as a narrator speaking in your own voice throughout, or (iii) by having the characters act it out directly (drama). **Epic uses the first mode — narration.** A single poet-narrator recounts events and characters' speech, rather than actors performing them live. This is the basic technical difference between epic and tragedy, even though (as we'll see) Aristotle treats them as close cousins in almost everything else.

---

## Hour 20: Epic Still Needs Unity of Plot

You might expect that because epic is narrated (not staged) and can be very long, it's free to simply chronicle "everything that happened" to a hero. Aristotle explicitly rejects this:

> "The plot manifestly ought, as in a tragedy, to be constructed on dramatic principles. It should have for its subject a single action, whole and complete, with a beginning, a middle, and an end."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XXIII

**In plain terms:** epic is not exempt from the same unity-of-plot rule we saw governing tragedy (Topic a) — it must still center on *one* connected action, not just be a list of things that happened to one man over his whole life. Aristotle praises Homer specifically for resisting the temptation to cover the entire Trojan War:

> "He never attempts to make the whole war of Troy the subject of his poem... as it is, he detaches a single portion, and admits as episodes many events from the general story."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XXIII

**In plain terms:** the *Iliad* doesn't tell the whole ten-year war — it focuses on one dispute (Achilles' quarrel with Agamemnon) and uses the rest of the war only as background material. Other epic poets of Aristotle's time (the "cyclic" poets) tried to cover an entire life or an entire war and, in Aristotle's judgment, produced weaker, less unified poems as a result.

## Hour 21: What Epic Can Do That Tragedy Can't

Because epic is narrated rather than performed live, it has some freedoms tragedy doesn't:

> "In Tragedy we cannot imitate several lines of actions carried on at one and the same time... But in Epic poetry, owing to the narrative form, many events simultaneously transacted can be presented."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XXIV

**In plain terms:** a stage can only show one scene at a time, but a narrator can freely say "meanwhile, elsewhere..." and cut between simultaneous events — something closer to what a novel or a film with parallel editing can do.

Epic also has more room for the strange and the wondrous:

> "The irrational, on which the wonderful depends for its chief effects, has wider scope in Epic poetry, because there the person acting is not seen."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XXIV

**In plain terms:** an implausible event (a god intervening unseen, an impossible feat) is easier to accept when it's *described* to us by a narrator than when we'd have to watch actors physically perform it in front of us. Distance from direct sight makes the audience more forgiving of the marvellous.

## Hour 22: The Meter of Epic

Epic poetry, in the Greco-Roman tradition, was composed in a specific verse form — the "heroic" meter, i.e. dactylic hexameter (the six-beat line used by Homer and Virgil). Aristotle notes, in *Poetics* Ch. XXIV, that this meter became attached to epic through long practical experience with what suited it best, rather than by any early theoretical rule. **In plain terms:** poets didn't sit down and *decide* hexameter was correct for epic in advance — generations of trial and audience response settled on it as the meter that best fit epic's scale, pacing, and elevated subject matter, and it stuck.

## Hour 23: Horace's Craft Advice — Don't Start "From the Egg"

Horace, writing generations after Aristotle, gives very concrete, practical advice on *how* to open an epic well. First, he mocks the bombastic epic opener that promises grand events in advance:

> "Don't open like the cyclic, with a burst: 'Troy's war and Priam's fate are here rehearsed.' What's coming, pray, that thus he winds his horn? The mountain labours, and a mouse is born."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** a poet who opens with huge, trumpeting promises ("I will sing of Troy's whole war and Priam's fate!") sets an expectation of something monumental — and then usually fails to deliver anything that big. "The mountain labours, and a mouse is born" is Horace's famous put-down for exactly this kind of anticlimax.

He contrasts this with Homer's actual opening of the *Odyssey*:

> "Far better he who enters at his ease, nor takes your breath with empty flourishes: 'Sing, Muse, the man who, after Troy was burned, saw divers cities, and their manners learned.'"
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

Horace also praises how Homer plunges straight into the action rather than narrating events in strict chronological order from the very start:

> "He sings not, he, of Diomed's return, starting from Meleager's funeral urn, nor when he tells the Trojan story, begs attention first for Leda and her eggs... He hurries to the crisis, lets you fall where facts crowd thick, as though you knew them all."
> — Horace, *Ars Poetica*, trans. Conington

**In plain terms:** Homer doesn't start "from the egg" — that is, from the mythical origin of the whole chain of events (Helen was hatched from an egg laid by Leda, according to myth) — he instead starts *in the middle* of the story and fills in background only as needed. This passage is the historical origin of the literary term **"in medias res"** ("into the midst of things"), still used today to describe any story that opens mid-action rather than at the chronological beginning.

## Hour 24: Is Tragedy or Epic the Higher Art? Aristotle's Verdict

The *Poetics* ends by directly comparing the two genres, and Aristotle comes down firmly on one side:

> "If tragedy is superior to epic poetry in all these respects, and, moreover, fulfills its specific function better as an art... it plainly follows that tragedy is the higher art, as attaining its end more perfectly."
> — *Poetics*, Ch. XXVI

**In plain terms:** Aristotle's reasoning (laid out earlier in Ch. XXVI, not fully quoted here) is that tragedy achieves everything epic achieves — a serious, unified plot, characters, catharsis — but does so more *concentrated*: in performance, with music and spectacle added, and within a tighter, more unified structure, without needing epic's greater length. For Aristotle, a shorter work that produces the same effect more intensely is the better-made work — so tragedy, not epic, is ranked as the more perfect literary achievement.

---

## Quick Recap

- Epic imitates through **narration** (a poet-narrator), unlike tragedy's direct dramatic performance.
- Epic still requires **unity of plot** — one connected action, not a full biography (Homer vs. the "cyclic" poets).
- Epic can show simultaneous events and tolerates more of the marvellous/irrational, because it is described rather than seen.
- Epic settled, through practice, on the "heroic" meter (hexameter).
- Horace: avoid bombastic openings ("the mountain labours, and a mouse is born"); prefer starting **in medias res**, as Homer does, not "from the egg."
- Aristotle's final verdict in the *Poetics*: tragedy is the higher art because it achieves the same effect as epic more economically and intensely.
